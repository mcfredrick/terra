## Today's Synthesis

The Southwest's 19% year-over-year hydro drop is a stress test you can run against your own models today. Pair the [record-low hydropower data](https://cleantechnica.com/2026/11/13/hydropower-generation-in-the-southwest-fell-to-record-lows-this-summer/) with the cost structure in the [curbside organics analysis](https://cleantechnica.com/2026/11/13/comparative-cost-analysis-for-residential-curbside-organics-collection-programs/) and you get a tractable forecasting problem: when hydro output collapses, the marginal generator is almost always gas, so every diesel-heavy, schedulable load — collection routes, depot charging — becomes both a cost line and an emissions lever, and the organics analysis gives you per-ton cost baselines to price that trade-off against. Concretely: build a 12-month probabilistic hydro shortfall forecast from ~40 years of Bureau of Reclamation reservoir elevation and streamflow data (gradient-boosted trees or a small temporal model), then wrap a dispatch optimizer around it that shifts EV fleet charging, including the depots behind services like [Zoox's Las Vegas expansion](https://cleantechnica.com/2026/11/13/zoox-expands-robotaxi-service-in-las-vegas/), into high-solar hours and re-sequences organics collection routes when the forecast says gas sets the margin. Validate by backtesting against April–August 2026 as a held-out drought. The datasets are public, the objective function is well-defined, and the output is something a fleet or public-works operator can actually consume.
